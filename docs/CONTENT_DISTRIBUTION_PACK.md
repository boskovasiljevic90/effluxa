## Post 1 — duplicate invoice review

Duplicate invoices are not always exact copies.

They can appear as:

- the same vendor and amount on nearby dates;
- a changed invoice number with the same line pattern;
- a credit note that was not connected to the original charge;
- a recurring charge that the team stopped expecting to see.

The safe workflow is not “AI found fraud”. It is:

1. identify a review signal;
2. show the source rows or pages;
3. compare the supporting documents;
4. let a person decide what happened;
5. record a verified outcome separately from a possible exposure.

That distinction is the difference between useful control work and an unsubstantiated savings claim.

## Post 2 — vendor price creep

Vendor price creep is difficult to notice when nobody compares like with like.

A practical review should normalise:

- vendor identity;
- item or service description;
- quantity and unit price;
- currency and tax treatment;
- effective date;
- contract or renewal context.

Only then can a team ask whether a price change is justified, approved or simply unnoticed.

The output should be a review queue with evidence—not a claim that every price difference is recoverable.

## Post 3 — for accounting firms

Accounting firms already have the client context. The missing piece is often a repeatable first-pass review process.

An additional leakage review layer should make it easier to:

- start from a client-approved export or document;
- connect a finding to source evidence;
- distinguish potential exposure from verified recovery;
- assign a follow-up owner;
- return to the next reporting period and compare what changed.

That workflow should support professional judgment, not try to replace it.

## Post 4 — responsible AI positioning

The most important sentence in an AI finance report may be the limitation.

“Potential duplicate exposure” is not the same as “confirmed loss”.

“Avoidable-cost run-rate” is not the same as “realised savings”.

“Low confidence” is not a result that should be presented as fact.

A responsible workflow keeps the evidence, confidence, calculation basis and reviewer decision close together. That makes the result more useful in a real control conversation—and easier to challenge when the source data is incomplete.
